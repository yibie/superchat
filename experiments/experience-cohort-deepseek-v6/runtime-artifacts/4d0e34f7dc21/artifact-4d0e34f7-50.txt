# Analyst Report: Conditions for Deterministic Replay in Event-Sourced Systems Under Schema Evolution

## Research Question

What conditions are required for deterministic replay in event-sourced systems when schemas evolve?

## Source Assessment

The collector's artifact draws from a strong body of primary sources. Below I assess each category of evidence, distinguish what is directly attested from what is inferred, and flag gaps.

### Tier 1 — Authoritative Specifications (Direct Evidence)

**Akka Data Integrity Exit Conditions** [1] provide the most formal and testable specification of conditions. Each condition is paired with a concrete, verifiable exit criterion. These are engineering specifications, not peer-reviewed research, but they are maintained by Lightbend as part of a commercial framework with a large production base. The conditions C1–C13 from the collector are directly attested.

**Gregory Young, _Versioning in an Event Sourced System_** [2] is the canonical practitioner text. Young's statements are derived from experience as architect of Event Store and a primary figure in event sourcing. His claims about weak schema, upcasting, and the convertibility requirement ("a new version must be convertible from the old version, or it is a new event") are direct and widely cited. However, this is a practitioner book (leanpub), not peer-reviewed research. The claims are authoritative within the community but lack formal empirical validation.

### Tier 2 — Peer-Reviewed Empirical Research (Strong Evidence)

**Overeem et al. (2021), JSS** [3] is the only peer-reviewed, empirical study of schema evolution in event-sourced systems. Based on grounded theory from 25 engineer interviews covering 19 systems. The five-tactic taxonomy (versioned events, weak schema, upcasting, in-place transformation, copy-and-transform) is directly evidenced. The finding that 8/19 systems use strict immutability, 3/19 use cut-off moments, and 8/19 allow mutation is important context: many real systems violate the immutability preconditions that the Akka spec assumes. This is a major insight — the theoretical conditions for deterministic replay are more stringent than what many production systems actually enforce.

### Tier 3 — Framework Documentation (Implementation Guidance)

**Axon Framework** [7], **Protean** [6], **Hexalith** [5], **noddde** [9], and **EventSourcingDB** documentation all describe consistent patterns for upcasting, version tagging, and pure handlers. These converge on the same principles, increasing confidence. However, framework docs are aspirational — they describe what the framework enables, not what practitioners always achieve.

### Tier 4 — Grey Literature (Valuable but Uneven)

The blog posts by Kevin Webber [10], event-driven.io [12], youngju.dev [13], ADHDecode [14], and the NILUS replay isolation article [15] are practitioner insights. They contain genuine experiential knowledge but should be treated as informed commentary, not verified findings.

## Analysis of Conditions

### Baseline Conditions (C1–C6): Schema-Static Deterministic Replay

The collector correctly identifies six baseline conditions. I note the following analytical points:

**C1 (Pure event handlers) and C2 (No wall-clock/randomness):** These are the two most frequently violated conditions in practice. The Overeem et al. study identifies 8/19 systems with mutable event stores, indicating that even event immutability (C3) is not universally enforced. The NILUS article [15] describes "hidden side effects" as a primary failure mode: "A consumer writes to an 'internal' cache that actually feeds a customer portal." The evidence strongly supports C1 and C2 as necessary conditions, but the empirical data shows they are difficult to enforce completely.

**C5 (Total event handler coverage):** The Hexalith "error-first contract" — throwing `UnknownEventException` rather than silently skipping — is a stronger form of this condition than what Akka specifies. The collector correctly captures this. A silent skip would produce incorrect aggregate state while appearing to succeed, which is more dangerous than an explicit failure.

**C6 (Separation of decide/evolve):** The Decider pattern (Chassaing) and the Mealy/Moore distinction (Webber) are conceptually elegant but the collector presents them as a "condition" when they are more accurately a design pattern that enables the first two conditions. The distinction matters: C6 is not independently necessary if C1 and C2 are satisfied by other means. I would recategorize C6 as a sufficient implementation strategy, not a necessary condition.

### Schema Evolution Conditions (C7–C13): My Assessment

**C7 (Backward compatibility of old events):** This is universally attested across all sources and is genuinely necessary. Gregory Young's formulation is the strongest: if an old event cannot be converted to the new version, then conceptually you have a new event, not a new version. There is no dissent on this point.

**C8 (Upcaster coverage for every version):** Necessary given the upcasting strategy, but note: the Overeem et al. study shows this is only one of five tactics. If a system uses weak schema (tolerant reader) exclusively, C8 is unnecessary — old events are read directly by current code. The Akka spec assumes an upcasting strategy, which is not universal.

**C9 (Version tagging):** Necessary for any approach that distinguishes versions. The collector correctly notes this, but should also note that in a purely weak-schema approach (JSON with tolerant reader), explicit version tags may be unnecessary — the absence of a field implies the old version. However, Young still recommends weak-schema systems include version markers for safety.

**C10 (No breaking type changes without upcasters):** Directly attested from Akka spec. The Protean compatibility matrix formalizes this in helpful detail.

**C11 (Serialization format choice):** This is more of a design consideration than a condition. The collector's summary of IDL-based vs. weak-schema tradeoffs is accurate. A key inference: the serialization format determines _which_ evolution operations are breaking, but does not change the fundamental requirement that old events must be readable. The format constrains the cost of satisfying C7, not its necessity.

**C12 (Snapshot compatibility):** The youngju.dev article [13] provides a real-world failure case: "A team refactored the Order aggregate... After deployment, all orders with existing snapshots failed to load." This confirms C12 as genuinely necessary in practice. The Akka condition `DATA-SNAPSHOT-MATCHES-REPLAY` is directly testable and failure consequences are severe.

**C13 (Idempotent projections):** Necessary for correctness of replay-based projection rebuilds. The collector correctly notes this, and the youngju.dev production anti-patterns article confirms that missing idempotency is a common production incident cause.

## Gaps and Contradictions

### Gap 1: The Upcasting vs. Weak Schema Tension

The Akka specification assumes upcasting as the primary mechanism. Gregory Young explicitly recommends _against_ strong-type-based versioning and favors weak schema (tolerant reader). The Overeem et al. study found _both_ approaches in production and recommends starting with versioned events + weak schema, then evolving to upcasting. The conditions presented by the collector are largely drawn from the upcasting-oriented Akka spec. Conditions C8 and C9 are specifically upcasting requirements, not universal. A system using only weak schema would need C7, C10, C11 but not C8 or C9.

### Gap 2: Semantic Drift Is Underrepresented

The NILUS article [15] identifies a critical gap: "Historical events often need upcasting, enrichment, or contextual remapping before they can be understood by a new model. This is not merely schema evolution. It is semantic evolution." The collector's conditions are syntax-level: can we deserialize, can we upcast, can we map fields. But deterministic replay can silently succeed (producing byte-identical state) while producing semantically wrong results if reference data, business rules, or regulatory interpretations have changed. This is an area where the Akka spec conditions are necessary but not sufficient.

### Gap 3: Empirical Prevalence of Condition Violations

The Overeem et al. finding that 8/19 systems allow mutable events is a significant contradiction to C3 (event immutability). If nearly half of production ESSs violate immutability, then deterministic replay as formally specified is not being achieved in those systems. The "cut-off moments" approach (3/19 systems) is a pragmatic compromise: immutability holds within operational windows but is violated during planned migrations. This suggests that in practice, many systems accept non-deterministic replay as a tradeoff for operational flexibility.

### Gap 4: No Source on Formal Verification

None of the sources provide formal verification that all conditions together are sufficient for deterministic replay. The Akka conditions are presented as exit criteria for testing, but there is no proof of completeness. This is an inference gap: we have strong evidence that each condition is necessary, but formal sufficiency is unproven.

### Contradiction: Double Publish as an Alternative

Gregory Young discusses "Double Publish" (writing both old and new versions) and notes it "works reasonably well" in stable situations but "will fail when you need to replay a projection." This is a nuanced point: what Young considers a failure mode, some practitioners may accept as a tolerable constraint. The contradiction is not about facts but about risk tolerance.

## Conclusion

The collector's artifact provides a comprehensive and well-sourced enumeration of conditions. As the analyst, I find the evidence quality strongest for C1–C5, C7, C10, C12, and C13, which are directly attested by formal specifications and corroborated by empirical study and production failure cases. C6 and C11 are design guidance rather than strict necessary conditions. C8 and C9 are necessary only within an upcasting strategy, not universally.

The most significant analytical finding is that _semantic drift_ — where events are syntactically replayable but semantically misinterpreted by evolved code — is a gap in the existing condition set. The Akka conditions guarantee syntactic determinism (byte-identical state) but do not guarantee semantic correctness. The NILUS replay isolation framework [15] is the only source that directly addresses this: replay should produce "candidate truth" requiring reconciliation, not assumed truth.

The Overeem et al. finding that nearly half of production ESSs violate event immutability is also significant: it suggests that many real-world systems operate with weaker guarantees than the theoretical conditions require, accepting non-deterministic replay as a tradeoff against operational constraints.

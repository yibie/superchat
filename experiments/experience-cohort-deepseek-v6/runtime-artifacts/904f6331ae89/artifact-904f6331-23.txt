# Capability-Based Authority vs. Identity-Based Access Control at Delegation Boundaries
## Analyst Artifact — Security-Provenance Family, disc-security-01

### Role: Analyst
### Sources Analyzed: Rajani, Garg & Rezk (CSF 2016); Hardy (ACM SIGOPS 1988); Murray & Grove (JCS 2008); Miller, Stiegler et al. (2003); Saltzer & Schroeder (Proc. IEEE 1975)

---

## 1. The Core Structural Difference

The fundamental distinction between capability-based authority systems and identity-based access control (IBAC, including ACLs) at delegation boundaries is captured by Miller et al.'s **Property A — "No Designation Without Authority"**: a capability serves simultaneously as both the designator of a resource and the authority to access it [Miller et al. 2003]. In IBAC/ACL systems, designation and authorization travel **separate paths**: a subject names the resource via a shared namespace (e.g., a filename string), and the system separately checks the subject's identity against an access control list stored with the resource. This separation is the root cause of all delegation-boundary differences between the two models.

**Evidence (Rajani et al. 2016 §II):** The authors formalize this in a region calculus with dual semantics. The access control semantics (ACs) "intervenes on every use (read/write) of a reference and checks that the use is compliant with a given access policy." The capability semantics (Cs) "intervenes on every transmitted and computed value and checks that if the value is a reference (capability), then the executing principal is authorized to use the capability according to the access policy." The critical difference is temporal: ACs checks at use-time; Cs checks at acquisition-time. Theorem 1 proves ACs is strictly more permissive than Cs — programs that ACs allows to execute are blocked by Cs because Cs prevents a principal from ever obtaining a capability it is not authorized to wield.

## 2. How Delegation Works in Each Model

### 2.1 Identity-Based (ACL) Delegation

In ACL systems, delegation is mediated through the **"grant" right** — a separate permission dimension that controls whether a subject may further propagate a permission to others. Saltzer & Schroeder (1975) define this as *propagation*: "When a principal, having been authorized access to some object, in turn authorizes access to another principal." The delegating principal's identity is continuously referenced; the system checks identity against the ACL at each access.

**Inference from evidence:** Because ACL delegation is identity-mediated and hierarchical, it naturally supports *non-delegatable grants* — one can grant Alice read access to a file without granting her the right to further delegate that access. This is a natural consequence of keeping the "grant" right separate from the access right in the policy specification.

**Source URL:** https://web.mit.edu/Saltzer/www/publications/protection/

### 2.2 Capability-Based Delegation

In a capability system, delegation is an **overt act of capability passing**. The delegator must hold both a capability to the resource *and* a capability to the delegatee (in object-capability systems, per Property F of Miller et al.: "Delegation on Access-Controlled Channels"). There is no ambient authority. The delegatee cannot exercise authority it has not been explicitly given.

**Evidence (Hardy 1988):** "The capability solution would endow the compiler with a direct capability to the statistics file. Instead of referring to the name of the file, the compiler would merely designate that capability when depositing the statistics. The capability both identifies the file and authorizes the compiler to write there. When producing the debugging output the compiler would merely refer to a capability provided by the invoker to the place he meant to hold that output."

## 3. The Delegation Boundary: Three Critical Dimensions

### 3.1 Confused Deputy Prevention

The confused deputy problem (Hardy 1988) is the paradigmatic delegation-boundary failure. In Hardy's account, a compiler with "home files license" authority over directory SYSX receives a filename "(SYSX)BILL" from its invoker — a file the invoker cannot access. The compiler's *ambient authority* is applied to open this file, overwriting the billing information. The compiler "serves two masters and carries some authority from each to perform its respective duties. It has no way to keep them apart."

**Analysis:** The confused deputy is fundamentally a *delegation-by-designation* attack. The adversary does not hold authority to write BILL, but designates it anyway. In ACL systems, designation is unchecked — any principal can name any resource. The system only checks authorization at access time, applying the deputy's identity. In capability systems, the adversary cannot designate a resource it lacks a capability for, so the attack is structurally prevented.

**Refinement (Rajani et al. 2016 §III):** Capability semantics prevent CDAs caused by *explicit designation* (where the adversary passes a reference it should not have), but do *not* prevent CDAs caused by *implicit designation* (where the adversary influences values or control flow that affect writes to resources the deputy already holds capabilities for). Examples 2–4 in Rajani et al. demonstrate value attacks, implicit influence via conditionals, and initial-heap attacks — all cases where Cs fails to prevent CDAs. This is a formal limitation not previously recognized in the capability literature.

### 3.2 Confinement (Preventing Delegation)

**Evidence (Miller et al. 2003):** In object-capability systems, "in order for Alice to authorize Bob to access Carol, Alice must herself be authorized to access both Bob and Carol." Confinement is compositional: "a subgraph of cooperating objects cannot delegate to Bob if Bob is not reachable from that subgraph." To prevent Alice from delegating to Bob, simply refrain from giving Alice a capability to Bob.

**Evidence (Miller, "Prohibiting Delegation"):** ACLs have an "expressiveness hole on confinement: not only can they not enforce the needed prohibitions, they cannot even express these prohibitions." Capability systems can express and enforce a subset (capability confinement).

**Analysis:** This is a structural advantage of capabilities at delegation boundaries. Confinement in capability systems is achieved by controlling the *connectivity graph* — it is geometric rather than policy-based. In ACL systems, confinement requires an administrator to anticipate all possible communication paths and encode prohibitions in policy, which is infeasible.

**Source URL:** http://erights.org/elib/capability/delegations.html

### 3.3 Non-Delegatable Authority

**Evidence (Murray & Grove 2008):** The NDA technique exploits "authority by method return" — the influence an invoked object has over its invoker through return values. "Since the general right to reply is not delegatable in many object-capability systems (E, EROS, W7), the NDA binds authority to a specific holder's identity." On each invocation, NDA calls back to the holder to obtain the method and parameters; the holder must actively collaborate on every invocation and cannot delegate the underlying authority.

**Analysis:** This is significant because it demonstrates that the perceived weakness of capabilities — that once given, they can be freely delegated — is surmountable. NDA effectively implements identity-based access control *within* the object-capability model. Murray and Grove show that NDAs can express ACL-like controls (subject lists with per-method permissions) and credential-based clearance systems. This means the capability model can subsume the non-delegation property that ACLs achieve naturally through the separate "grant" right.

**Inference:** The advantage of capabilities is not that they *cannot* do non-delegation, but that non-delegation is the *default* in ACLs (you must explicitly grant the "grant" right), while in capabilities it requires an explicit NDA pattern.

**Source URL:** https://www.cs.ox.ac.uk/files/2691/NDA.pdf (DOI: 10.3233/JCS-2008-0314)

## 4. Communicating Conspirators — The Unenforceable Boundary

**Evidence (Miller, "Prohibiting Delegation"):** For communicating conspirators (where two subjects collude out-of-band), "capabilities have an expressiveness hole: They cannot express this prohibition, and therefore they cannot enforce it. Unlike capabilities, ACLs can express this prohibition." However, "even with complete omniscience and omnipotence over the state of a system, no force in the universe can enforce this prohibition, since the only difference between what's allowed and what's prohibited is how we choose to describe it."

**Analysis:** This is arguably the deepest insight about delegation boundaries. ACLs create a *false sense of security* by allowing administrators to express prohibitions that cannot be enforced (preventing two colluding parties from sharing information out-of-band). Capabilities are honest about this limitation. Miller argues that "by leading users and programmers to make decisions under a false sense of security about what others can be prevented from doing, ACLs seduce them into actions that compromise their own security."

## 5. Formal Non-Equivalence

**Evidence (Rajani et al. 2016, Theorem 1):** ACs is strictly more permissive than Cs. The converse of the inclusion is false: there exist programs that ACs allows but Cs blocks. The counterexample is a program that stores a reference in a location where the executing region has write permission to the location but not to the referenced object. ACs allows this because the write is legitimate and the referenced capability is never *used*; Cs blocks it because the region must *compute* (possess) the capability, which it is not authorized to wield.

**Inference:** This is not merely an implementation choice — it is a fundamental semantic divergence. Capability systems enforce authorization at *acquisition time*; identity-based systems enforce it at *use time*. This has practical consequences: capability systems are more restrictive but provide stronger guarantees about what an executing program can do with the authority it holds.

## 6. Summary Comparison

| Dimension | Identity-Based (IBAC/ACL) | Capability-Based |
|---|---|---|
| **What crosses delegation boundary** | Name/designator; authority checked separately against identity | Capability that is both designator and authority |
| **Authorization timing** | Use-time (check identity against ACL at each access) | Acquisition-time (cannot receive unusable capability) |
| **Delegation mechanism** | "Grant" right in policy; ambient authority of delegatee | Explicit capability passing; requires capability to recipient (ocaps) |
| **Non-delegation** | Natural: omit "grant" right | Requires NDA pattern (authority by method return) |
| **Confinement** | Policy-based; cannot prevent conspirators | Structural/compositional via connectivity |
| **Confused deputy** | Inherent vulnerability (designation/authority separation) | Prevented for explicit designation; vulnerable to implicit influence |
| **Communicating conspirators** | Expresses prohibition → false sense of security | Cannot express the unenforceable → honest |
| **Formal permissiveness** | Strictly more permissive (Rajani et al. Theorem 1) | Strictly more restrictive |

## 7. Key Inferences

1. **The two models are not equivalent.** Rajani et al. (2016) provide formal proof. Any claim that capabilities and ACLs are "just rows vs. columns of the access matrix" (as Chander et al. suggested) fails when Property A is modeled — and Property A is what makes capability systems secure.

2. **The delegation boundary is where the models diverge most sharply.** Inside a single security domain, both models can achieve similar outcomes. But when authority crosses from one principal to another, the structural differences — designation/authority unification vs. separation, acquisition-time vs. use-time enforcement — produce fundamentally different security properties.

3. **Neither model is strictly superior.** Capabilities excel at confinement and confused-deputy prevention but require additional machinery (NDAs) for identity-bound non-delegation. ACLs naturally express non-delegation but are structurally vulnerable to confused deputies and cannot express confinement. The choice is about which failure modes are acceptable in a given domain.

4. **Modern systems are hybrid.** As noted by the Identity Foundation blog (2025), autonomous agent systems increasingly require both models: capabilities for attenuation and delegation chains, identity for accountability and provenance. The theoretical purity of either model yields to practical requirements at scale.

**Source URL (Identity Foundation):** https://blog.identity.foundation/building-ai-trust-at-scale-4/

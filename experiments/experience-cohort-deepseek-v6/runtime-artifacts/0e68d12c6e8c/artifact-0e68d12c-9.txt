## 1. The Two Complementary Lines of Evidence

The meta-research literature addresses the question through two complementary approaches:

**A. Detection studies** compare registrations/protocols to publications, measuring the prevalence and nature of discrepancies. These studies demonstrate what preregistration *enables*: without a public record, outcome switching is undetectable.

**B. Association studies** compare outcome distributions (p-values, effect sizes, bias indicators) between preregistered and non-preregistered studies to assess whether preregistration is associated with reduced evidence of selective reporting.

---

## 2. What Preregistration Enables: The Prevalence of Outcome Switching

### 2.1 Thibault et al. (2023) — Largest Systematic Review (k=89 articles)

Published in *BMJ Open*, this is the most comprehensive meta-analysis of discrepancy prevalence:

- **29%–37% (95% CI)** of studies had ≥1 primary outcome discrepancy between registration and publication (n=6,314 studies).
- **50%–75% (95% CI)** had ≥1 secondary outcome discrepancy (n=1,436).
- Specific types: primary outcome omitted (6–12%), demoted to secondary (6–10%), new primary added (7–11%).
- **Only 4%–19%** disclosed discrepancies. Only 1 interventional study identified.
- High heterogeneity (I²=86%) not explained by discipline, protocol format, or year.

**Source:** https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10551944/

### 2.2 Holst et al. (2023) — Registry Version-History Analysis

Published in *PLOS Medicine*, this analyzed the complete change-history of 1,746 RCTs completed at German university medical centers (2009–2017):

- **55%** had primary outcome changes at any timepoint (95% CI [49%, 61%]).
- **41%** had discrepancies between the latest registry entry and publication.
- **14%** had changes detectable *only* by inspecting historical registry versions — invisible if comparing publication to latest entry alone.
- Only **1%** (2 of 161) disclosed changes.
- Industry-sponsored trials had ~3.4× greater odds of within-registry outcome changes.

**Source:** https://journals.plos.org/plosmedicine/article?id=10.1371/journal.pmed.1004306

### 2.3 Ramagopalan et al. (2014) — ClinicalTrials.gov Cross-Section

Of 89,204 interventional trials on ClinicalTrials.gov:

- **31.7%** had their primary outcome changed.
- **30.3%** changed after the study start date; **30.4%** after primary completion date.
- Industry funding associated with all changes (OR=1.36, 95% CI 1.31–1.41) and changes after completion (OR=1.84, 95% CI 1.75–1.94).

**Source:** https://f1000research.com/articles/3-77

### 2.4 Kampman et al. (2021) — Follow-Up Publications

Of 78 follow-up publications in NEJM, Lancet, JAMA, and BMJ (2014–2018):

- **40%** used different primary outcomes compared with the original RCT.
- **55%** of switches were neither pre-specified nor explained.
- Outcome switching in follow-ups rose to **70%** when preceded by switching in the original RCT (p<0.001).

**Source:** https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jclinepi.2021.05.003

### 2.5 Song et al. (2026) — Observational Cohort Studies

Longitudinal meta-epidemiological study of 124 controlled cohort studies on ClinicalTrials.gov:

- Only **24%** completely pre-specified primary outcomes (domain, metric, aggregation method, time point).
- **48%** had outcome switching; only 2 provided an explanation.
- **77%** of switches favored statistically significant results.

**Source:** https://doi.org/10.1136/bmj-2025-087975

### 2.6 Tricco et al. (2016) — Systematic Review Registrations (PROSPERO)

Of 96 published systematic reviews registered in PROSPERO:

- **32%** had a primary outcome discrepancy.
- **39%** did not explicitly specify a primary outcome in the review.
- **6%** omitted a protocol-specified primary outcome entirely.

**Source:** https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jclinepi.2016.03.025

## 3. Evidence That Preregistration Is Associated with Reduced Bias

### 3.1 Decker & Ottaviani (2023) — p-Hacking in Clinical Trials

Analyzed p-value distributions from pre-approval drug trials on ClinicalTrials.gov (CEPR DP18180):

- **Non-preregistered trials:** z-score density showed a significant upward discontinuity at p=0.05 (the classic p-hacking signature). The density was 54% larger on the significant side of the threshold.
- **Preregistered trials:** density was smooth at p=0.05 — no evidence of p-hacking.
- Robust to sponsor fixed effects, blinding status, and data monitoring committees.

This provides the strongest available evidence that preregistration is associated with reduced selective reporting in clinical trials, though the authors explicitly note the absence of exogenous variation precludes definitive causal claims.

**Source:** https://cepr.org/publications/dp18180

### 3.2 Brodeur et al. (2024) — Preregistration vs. Preanalysis Plans in Economics

Analyzed 15,992 test statistics from all RCTs in 15 leading economics journals (*JPE Microeconomics*, 2024):

- **Preregistration alone (without a preanalysis plan):** no evidence of reduced p-hacking or publication bias.
- **Preregistration with a preanalysis plan (PAP):** evidence consistent with reduced p-hacking and reduced publication bias. PAP studies had ~10 percentage points fewer marginally significant test statistics at the 5% threshold.
- Relative publication probability (significant vs. insignificant): 1.38 for PAP studies vs. 1.65 for preregistered-without-PAP.

**Key implication:** The *content* of preregistration matters decisively. Vague registrations that do not constrain researcher degrees of freedom confer little protection.

**Source:** https://doi.org/10.1086/730455

### 3.3 Chen et al. (2019) — Outcome Change and Intervention Effect Size

Cross-sectional study of 389 RCTs with clear prospectively registered primary outcomes (*JAMA Network Open*):

- **33.4%** had ≥1 primary outcome change.
- Trials with primary outcome changes reported **16% larger intervention effect sizes** (pooled ROR=0.84, 95% CI 0.73–0.96), persisting after adjustment (ROR=0.81, 95% CI 0.71–0.93).

**Source:** https://doi.org/10.1001/jamanetworkopen.2019.7242

### 3.4 Tian et al. (2025) — Prospective Registration and Risk of Bias

Meta-research of 2,030 RCTs from 151 systematic reviews (*J Clin Epidemiol*):

- Prospectively registered trials were more likely than nonregistered trials to have adequate randomization (OR=1.51), allocation concealment (OR=1.69), blinded outcome assessment (OR=1.65), and lower overall risk of bias (OR=2.04).
- Prospective vs. retrospective registration comparisons favored prospective but were not statistically significant for overall RoB.

**Source:** https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jclinepi.2025.111813

## 4. Evidence from Psychology: Preregistration Alone Is Insufficient

### 4.1 Van den Akker et al. (2023) — Effectiveness of Preregistration

Compared 193 preregistered psychology studies (badged/prize-winning) with 193 matched non-preregistered studies (*Behavior Research Methods*):

- **No difference** in proportion of positive results (β=0.01, 99% CI [−0.56, 0.59], p=.96) or effect sizes (β=−0.04, p=.18).
- **No difference** in statistical errors (p=.08).
- Preregistered studies did have larger sample sizes (959.6 vs. 536.6, p<.001) and more power analyses.

**Source:** https://doi.org/10.3758/s13428-023-02277-0

### 4.2 Van den Akker et al. (2023) — Selective Hypothesis Reporting

Compared 459 preregistrations with publications (*AMPPS*):

- **52%** omitted hypotheses; **57%** added hypotheses; **18%** changed hypothesis direction.
- **60%** of studies had ≥1 form of selective hypothesis reporting.
- Added hypotheses were more likely to be statistically significant (OR=2.11, 99% CI [1.15, 3.86]).

**Source:** https://doi.org/10.1177/25152459231187988

### 4.3 Sandoval-Lentisco et al. (2025) — Psychology Meta-Analyses

Of 1,403 psychology meta-analyses published in 2021, only **27%** were preregistered. Among 100 randomly sampled preregistered meta-analyses (*AMPPS*):

- **All 100** contained ≥1 deviation from protocol (median=9).
- **Median of 8 deviations were undisclosed.**
- Median of only 13 of 23 PRISMA-P items were covered in protocols.

**Source:** https://doi.org/10.1177/25152459241300113

## 5. Synthesis: What the Evidence Does and Does Not Support

### Empirical findings (directly supported by data):

1. **Outcome discrepancies are pervasive:** 29–37% primary outcome discrepancy prevalence (Thibault 2023 meta-analysis); up to 55% when full registry histories are inspected (Holst 2023).
2. **Disclosure is vanishingly rare:** 1–19% across studies.
3. **Discrepancies bias results in predictable directions:** Outcome switching is associated with 16% larger reported effects (Chen 2019); 77% of switches favor statistical significance (Song 2026).
4. **Preregistration signals reduced p-hacking in clinical trials:** Smooth p-value distributions at significance thresholds for preregistered trials vs. pronounced discontinuities for non-preregistered (Decker & Ottaviani 2023).
5. **Registration detail is decisive:** Preregistration alone shows no evidence of bias reduction (Brodeur 2024 in economics; Van den Akker 2023 in psychology); preregistration with detailed preanalysis plans does.
6. **Preregistration does not eliminate deviations:** Even among preregistered studies, deviations from protocols are universal (Sandoval-Lentisco 2025: 100/100 meta-analyses had deviations).
7. **Preregistration enables detection:** 14% of outcome changes are invisible without inspecting full registry histories (Holst 2023).

### Inferential claims (plausible but not causally demonstrated):

- That preregistration *causes* reduced selective reporting rather than being confounded by researcher conscientiousness or institutional quality.
- That mandatory registration policies reduce outcome switching at scale (only 1 interventional study identified; Thibault 2023).
- That findings from clinical medicine generalize to psychology, economics, and other disciplines (evidence from non-clinical fields is sparser and inconsistent).

---

## 6. Key Limitations and Gaps (Assessed Across Sources)

| Gap | Evidence |
|-----|----------|
| **Causal evidence** | Only 1 interventional study across 89 articles (Thibault 2023) |
| **Discipline concentration** | Nearly all meta-research from clinical medicine |
| **Underestimation of prevalence** | Comparing publications to latest registry entries misses ~14% of changes (Holst 2023) |
| **Preregistration quality vs. existence** | Many registrations lack sufficient detail to constrain researcher degrees of freedom (Brodeur 2024; Sandoval-Lentisco 2025) |
| **Registered Reports** | Direct comparative evidence between Registered Reports and standard preregistration remains limited |

## Reviewer Conclusion

The evidence converges on a clear finding: preregistration enables detection of outcome switching and is associated with reduced evidence of p-hacking in clinical trials, but it does not eliminate the problem. Deviations from preregistered plans are pervasive across all studied disciplines, most go undisclosed, and the protective effect of preregistration depends critically on the specificity of the registered plan. The strongest causal inference comes from Decker & Ottaviani (2023) using sponsor fixed effects, but definitive causal evidence remains sparse. The field would benefit from experimental interventions testing methods to reduce discrepancies and from expanded meta-research outside clinical medicine.

# Evidence That Study Preregistration Changes Selective Reporting or Outcome Switching: A Meta-Research Summary

## 1. Overview

Preregistration — the practice of documenting study plans in a permanent, time-stamped, publicly accessible record before data collection or analysis — is intended to reduce selective reporting and outcome switching. A substantial body of primary meta-research now quantifies both the baseline prevalence of these practices and the effects of preregistration. This summary draws exclusively on published primary meta-research and systematic reviews.

**Key finding:** Preregistration enables *detection* of selective reporting and is associated with reduced evidence of p-hacking; however, preregistration alone does not eliminate outcome switching — discrepancies between registrations and publications remain common, and most deviations go undisclosed.

---

## 2. Prevalence of Outcome Discrepancies (Baseline Evidence)

### Systematic review and meta-analysis across disciplines

Thibault et al. (2023), *BMJ Open*, conducted the largest systematic review to date, reviewing k=89 articles covering over 7,000 studies. Their random-effects meta-analyses found:

- **29%–37% (95% CI)** of studies contained at least one primary outcome discrepancy between registration and publication.
- **50%–75% (95% CI)** contained at least one secondary outcome discrepancy.
- Specific discrepancy types: primary outcome omitted (6%–12%), primary outcome demoted to secondary (6%–10%), new primary outcome added (7%–11%), secondary outcome promoted to primary (4%–6%).

Critically, only **4%–19%** of studies with discrepancies disclosed them. Nearly all evidence came from clinical research; data from psychology, economics, and other disciplines was sparse.

**Source:** https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10551944/

### Large-scale ClinicalTrials.gov cross-section

Ramagopalan et al. (2014), *F1000Research*, analyzed all 89,204 interventional trials registered on ClinicalTrials.gov as of October 2012:

- **31.7%** (28,229 trials) had their primary outcome changed.
- **30.3%** changed the primary outcome *after the study start date*.
- **30.4%** changed it after the primary completion date.
- Industry funding was associated with all primary outcome changes (OR=1.36, 95% CI 1.31–1.41) and changes after completion (OR=1.84, 95% CI 1.75–1.94).

**Source:** https://f1000research.com/articles/3-77

### Registry version-history analysis (German university medical centers)

Holst et al. (2023), *PLOS Medicine*, analyzed the complete change-history of 1,746 RCTs completed at German university medical centers (2009–2017):

- **55%** of trials had primary outcome changes at some point (95% CI [49%, 61%]).
- **23%** had *major* changes (primary outcomes added, dropped, or switched with secondary outcomes).
- **41%** had discrepancies between the latest registry entry and the publication.
- **14%** had changes detectable *only* by inspecting historical registry versions (invisible if comparing publication to the latest entry alone).
- Only **1%** of trials with changes (2 of 161) reported them in the publication.

Industry-sponsored trials were more likely to have within-registry outcome changes (OR 0.29 for non-industry, i.e., industry ~3.4× more likely). More recently registered trials had lower odds of discrepancies (OR 0.74 per year).

**Source:** https://journals.plos.org/plosmedicine/article/file?id=10.1371/journal.pmed.1004306&type=printable

### Systematic reviews and PROSPERO

Tricco et al. (2016), *Journal of Clinical Epidemiology*, compared PROSPERO records with published systematic reviews:

- **32%** had a primary outcome discrepancy.
- **39%** did not explicitly specify a primary outcome in the review.
- **6%** of reviews omitted a protocol-specified primary outcome entirely.

**Source:** https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jclinepi.2016.03.025

## 3. Evidence That Preregistration Reduces Selective Reporting

### P-hacking and z-score discontinuity

Decker & Ottaviani (2023), CEPR Discussion Paper 18180, analyzed p-value distributions from pre-approval drug trials on ClinicalTrials.gov, comparing preregistered vs. non-preregistered trials:

- **Non-preregistered trials:** The z-score density displayed a significant upward discontinuity at the p=0.05 threshold, the classic signature of p-hacking / selective reporting.
- **Preregistered trials:** The density was smooth at the p=0.05 threshold — no evidence of p-hacking.
- Results were robust when conditioning on sponsor fixed effects, blinding status, and data monitoring committees.

This provides direct evidence that preregistration is associated with reduced selective reporting / p-hacking in clinical trials.

**Source:** https://cepr.org/publications/dp18180

### Primary outcome change and intervention effect size

A cross-sectional study (Chen et al., 2019, *JAMA*-affiliated) of 389 RCTs with clear prospectively registered primary outcomes found:

- **33.4%** had at least one primary outcome change.
- Trials with primary outcome changes showed a **16% larger reported intervention effect size** (pooled ROR 0.84, 95% CI 0.73–0.96), persisting after adjustment (ROR 0.81, 95% CI 0.71–0.93).

**Source:** https://keele-repository.worktribe.com/output/414158

### Outcome switching in follow-up publications

A meta-research study of follow-up publications (2014–2018) in *NEJM*, *The Lancet*, *JAMA*, and *BMJ* found:

- **40%** of follow-up publications used different primary outcomes than the original RCT.
- In **55%** of these, the switch was neither pre-specified nor explained.
- Outcome switching in follow-ups rose to **70%** when preceded by switching in the original RCT (p<0.001).

**Source:** https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0895435621001463

### Outcome switching in observational cohort studies

A 2026 longitudinal meta-epidemiological study of 124 controlled cohort studies registered on ClinicalTrials.gov (2014–2016) found:

- Only **24%** completely pre-specified their primary outcomes (domain, metric, aggregation method, time point).
- **48%** had outcome switching; only 2 studies provided an explanation.
- **77%** of switches favored statistically significant results.

**Source:** https://portal.findresearcher.sdu.dk/da/publications/outcome-switching-in-cohort-studies-of-interventions-meta-epidemi/

## 4. Evidence from Psychology and Non-Clinical Disciplines

### Psychology meta-analyses

Sandoval-Lentisco et al. (2025), *Advances in Methods and Practices in Psychological Science*, examined 1,403 psychology meta-analyses published in 2021:

- Only **27%** were preregistered.
- Among 100 randomly sampled preregistered meta-analyses, **all 100** contained at least one deviation from the protocol (median=9 deviations).
- **Median of 8 deviations were undisclosed** per meta-analysis.
- Key decisions were often omitted from protocols (median of 13 of 23 PRISMA-P items covered).

**Source:** https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/25152459241300113

### Prospective registration and review quality

Ge et al. (2019), a meta-epidemiological study, found that prospectively registered systematic reviews had significantly higher methodological quality (R-AMSTAR scores) than non-registered reviews (MD=4.82, 95% CI 3.70–5.94), suggesting indirect benefits of registration on overall rigor.

**Source:** https://ueaeprints.uea.ac.uk/id/eprint/65354/1/Accepted_manuscript.pdf

## 5. Factors Associated with Outcome Switching

| Factor | Evidence |
|---|---|
| **Industry funding** | Consistently associated with more primary outcome changes (Ramagopalan 2014; Holst 2023) |
| **Statistical significance** | Switches favor significant results: 77% of switches in cohort studies favored significance; trials with changes reported 16% larger effect sizes |
| **Registration recency** | Later registration years associated with fewer discrepancies (Holst 2023: OR 0.74/year) |
| **Registry platform** | ClinicalTrials.gov trials had more changes than DRKS-registered trials, possibly reflecting easier editing |
| **Disclosure** | Overwhelmingly rare: 1%–19% of changes reported across studies |

---

## 6. Synthesis: Inference vs. Evidence

**What the evidence directly supports (empirical findings):**
1. Outcome discrepancies between registrations and publications are common across medicine, psychology, and systematic reviews.
2. Most discrepancies are undisclosed.
3. Discrepancies tend to favor statistically significant results and are associated with larger reported effect sizes.
4. Preregistered trials show *smoother* p-value distributions at the significance threshold, consistent with less p-hacking.
5. Preregistration enables detection of selective reporting; without it, such detection is impossible.

**What is inferential (supported but not directly causally demonstrated):**
- That preregistration *causes* reduction in selective reporting (rather than being associated with it through confounders like researcher conscientiousness).
- That mandatory preregistration policies would reduce outcome switching at scale (only one intervention study identified by Thibault et al. 2023, with null results).
- That results from clinical medicine generalize fully to other disciplines (data remain sparse outside medicine).

---

## 7. Key Gaps Identified

- Very few intervention studies testing methods to reduce discrepancies (Thibault et al. identified only 1 out of 89 articles).
- Little evidence from disciplines outside clinical medicine.
- Most studies cannot distinguish undisclosed vs. disclosed discrepancies or prospective vs. retrospective registration.
- The fact that 14% of outcome changes are invisible when only comparing publications to latest registry entries (Holst 2023) means existing literature underestimates true prevalence.

**Primary sources consulted:** 11 peer-reviewed meta-research articles, systematic reviews, and meta-epidemiological studies, all with retained URLs above.

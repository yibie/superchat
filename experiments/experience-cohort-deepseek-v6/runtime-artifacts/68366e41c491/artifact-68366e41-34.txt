# Under What Conditions Do Registered Reports Reduce Publication Bias? Evidence from Primary Meta-Research

## Summary

Registered Reports (RRs) reduce publication bias under a specific, well-defined set of structural conditions. The primary meta-research evidence — drawn from comparative studies of published RR corpora, peer-review experiments, and comprehensive reviews — converges on several necessary and facilitative conditions. This Artifact synthesizes that evidence, distinguishing empirical findings from inferential claims.

---

## 1. The Core Mechanism: In-Principle Acceptance Before Results Are Known

The foundational condition for RRs to reduce publication bias is **in-principle acceptance (IPA)**: the journal's binding commitment to publish the study regardless of the direction, significance, or novelty of results, provided the approved protocol is followed (Chambers & Tzavella, *Nature Human Behaviour*, 2022). This two-stage review process — Stage 1 peer review of the question, theory, and methods before data collection, followed by Stage 2 review checking protocol adherence — structurally prevents editorial decisions from being influenced by results. As the CASRAI guide (2026) explains: "an editor cannot favor a statistically significant or novel-looking finding over a null one when no finding has happened yet."

The key distinction from mere preregistration is critical: preregistration documents what was planned but does not obligate any journal to publish null results. Only the IPA mechanism removes the incentive for authors to file-drawer negative findings and for editors to select for positive ones (Lakens et al., *Evidence-Based Toxicology*, 2024).

## 2. Empirical Evidence: Magnitude of the Effect

### Scheel, Schijen, & Lakens (2021) — "An Excess of Positive Results"

The landmark study compared 71 published RRs in psychology (as of November 2018) with 152 randomly sampled hypothesis-testing standard reports (SRs). Analyzing the first hypothesis of each article:

- **SRs: 96.05% positive results** (95% CI [91.61, 98.54])
- **RRs: 43.66% positive results** (95% CI [31.91, 55.95])
- Difference: −52.39 percentage points (χ²(1) = 77.96, p < .001)

When close replications were excluded, the RR positive rate was 50.00% — still dramatically below the standard literature. The authors conclude that "the numbers strongly suggest a reduction of publication bias and/or QRPs in the RR literature."

Source: *Advances in Methods and Practices in Psychological Science*, 4(2), 2021. https://doi.org/10.1177/25152459211007467

### Allen & Mehler (2019) — PLOS Biology

Surveyed 113 published biomedical and psychological science RRs (296 discrete hypotheses):

- **60.5% of hypotheses unsupported** (95% CI [54.7–66.1%])
- Replication attempts: 66.0% unsupported
- Novel research: 54.5% unsupported
- Compared to estimated 5–20% null findings in the traditional literature

Source: *PLOS Biology*, 17(5), e3000246. https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pbio.3000246

### Liu et al. (2026) — Meta-Evaluation in *American Psychologist*

Matched 119 pairs of RR and non-RR articles from 33 psychology journals. RRs "significantly reduced publication bias against null results and improved method and data transparency." This study controlled for journal, publication time, and research topic through matching.

Source: *American Psychologist*, 81(2), 254–269. https://doi.org/10.1037/amp0001503

## 3. Necessary Conditions for Bias Reduction

From the primary literature, the following conditions must hold for RRs to reduce publication bias:

### (a) The IPA Commitment Must Be Credible and Binding

If journals can renege on IPA at Stage 2 based on results (e.g., rejecting because findings are "not interesting"), the mechanism collapses. All RR-adopting journals specify that editorial decisions at Stage 2 "will not be based on the perceived importance, novelty, or conclusiveness of the results" (Nature Methods guidelines; PLOS Computational Biology, 2022). The commitment is conditional only on protocol adherence, quality checks, and defensible interpretation.

### (b) Stage 1 Review Must Occur Before Data Collection/Access

The temporal sequence is essential. RRs where authors have already accessed data — even partially — receive lower "bias control levels" under the PCI RR taxonomy (e.g., Level 2 vs. Level 6). The Chambers & Tzavella (2022) review notes that RRs are "best suited for instances where data is yet to be collected at the time of the first submission."

### (c) Protocol Adherence Must Be Verified at Stage 2

Stage 2 reviewers check whether the approved protocol was followed. Deviations must be disclosed and justified; undisclosed deviations can lead to rejection. This verification prevents authors from HARKing or p-hacking after seeing results, which would reintroduce bias despite the IPA structure.

### (d) Outcome-Neutral Quality Checks Must Be Built In

RRs typically require "positive controls," "manipulation checks," or "sanity checks" — tests that verify the study was capable of detecting an effect regardless of the hypothesis outcome. Without these, null results could be dismissed as methodological failures rather than genuine non-effects (PLOS Computational Biology, 10 Simple Rules, 2022).

### (e) Confirmatory and Exploratory Analyses Must Be Distinguished

RRs require clear separation: pre-registered confirmatory analyses carry the evidentiary weight; post hoc exploratory analyses are permitted but must be labeled as such. Without this distinction, authors could selectively present exploratory findings as confirmatory, undermining the bias-reduction mechanism (Chambers & Tzavella, 2022).

## 4. Facilitative Conditions That Strengthen the Effect

### High Statistical Power Requirements

Many RR-adopting journals require a priori power analyses (often ≥90% or ≥95% power). This reduces false negatives, making null results more informative and less likely to reflect underpowered designs rather than genuine absence of effects (Scheel et al., 2021; Nature RR guidelines).

### Public Protocol Registration

Most RR journals require or encourage registration of the accepted Stage 1 protocol in a public repository (e.g., OSF, figshare). This creates a permanent, time-stamped record, enabling independent verification of what was planned versus what was reported (Chambers & Mellor, *Nature Human Behaviour*, 2018).

### Peer Review Quality at Stage 1

The Soderberg et al. (2021) experiment found that RRs were rated higher on methodological rigor (mean difference 0.99 on a −4 to +4 scale, 95% CrI [0.62, 1.35]) and analysis quality (0.97, [0.60, 1.34]) compared to matched standard papers. Better methods reduce noise that could mask true effects, strengthening the interpretability of null results.

Source: *Nature Human Behaviour*, 5, 990–997 (2021). https://doi.org/10.1038/s41562-021-01142-4

## 5. Known Confounds and Limitations (Evidence of Inference, Not Fact)

The primary meta-research explicitly acknowledges several confounds that qualify causal claims:

1. **Selection into RR format**: Authors may use RRs strategically for hypotheses they expect to yield null results, or early-adopting authors may be unusually conscientious. Scheel et al. (2021) note this is plausible but insufficient to explain a 52-percentage-point gap.

2. **Editorial policy confound**: Journals offering RRs may have more progressive editorial policies that reduce bias across all article types. However, a within-journal comparison across three journals (n = 27 articles) showed the same pattern: 84.62% positive in SRs vs. 50.00% in RRs, suggesting the effect is not purely journal-level (Scheel et al., 2021).

3. **Small sample sizes**: As of 2021, only ~71–127 published RRs were available for analysis. Scheel et al. caution that reliability is limited by small samples; results may not generalize as the format scales.

4. **No randomized evidence**: All evidence is observational/comparative. Lakens et al. (2024) note that a randomized trial assigning researchers to RR vs. standard formats is "practically out of reach," making causal claims inherently inferential.

5. **Discipline specificity**: Evidence is concentrated in psychology and neuroscience. Chambers & Tzavella (2022) note that RRs "are not a universal solution for irreproducibility" and effects may differ in fields with weaker replication problems.

6. **Lower citation counts**: Liu et al. (2026) found RRs receive fewer citations than matched non-RRs (small effect when controlling for days since publication). This may create a countervailing incentive against RR adoption.

---

## 6. Conditions Under Which RRs Do NOT Reduce Publication Bias

- When IPA is not genuinely binding and editors still exercise results-based discretion
- When authors deviate substantially from protocols without disclosure
- For purely exploratory research where hypotheses cannot be meaningfully pre-specified
- When the format is used as a "badge" of credibility without rigorous Stage 1 review
- In fields where the replication crisis is weak or absent (the RR mechanism addresses a problem that may not exist equally everywhere)

# How a Systematic Review Should Represent Materially Contradictory Primary Studies Without Collapsing Uncertainty

## 1. The Core Problem

When primary studies in a systematic review yield materially contradictory findings (effects in opposite directions, or substantially different magnitudes), there is a well-documented risk that reviewers collapse this uncertainty into a single misleading conclusion — either by ignoring heterogeneity, using inappropriate synthesis methods, or over-interpreting a pooled average. The authoritative guidance collectively warns against this and prescribes a multi-layered approach.

---

## 2. GRADE Framework: Rating Down for Inconsistency, Not Pretending It Away

The GRADE Working Group's approach (Guyatt et al., 2023, *Journal of Clinical Epidemiology* 158:70–83) is the dominant framework for handling contradictory evidence. The key principle is that **unexplained inconsistency must reduce certainty, not be concealed**.

### 2.1 The Core GRADE Inconsistency Assessment (BMJ 2025, "Core GRADE 3")

- Reviewers must first decide whether inconsistency is **problematic** by examining: (i) the magnitude of differences in point estimates, (ii) the overlap of confidence intervals, and (iii) where point estimates lie relative to the chosen threshold (the null or the minimal important difference).
- If all point estimates fall on the same side of the threshold, inconsistency may not be problematic even if statistical heterogeneity is high.
- If point estimates lie on opposite sides of the threshold, the inconsistency is likely important.

### 2.2 A Priori Subgroup Hypotheses

- Before seeing results, reviewers should formulate **a small number (ideally ≤3) of directional a priori hypotheses** about population or intervention characteristics that might explain inconsistency (GRADE guidance 36; Guyatt et al., 2023).
- If a credible subgroup effect is found (assessed using the ICEMAN instrument), **separate evidence summaries and separate certainty ratings** should be provided for each subgroup — not a single collapsed conclusion.
- If no credible subgroup effect is found, the evidence is rated down for inconsistency. The single evidence summary then explicitly carries lower certainty.

### 2.3 GRADE Book Guidance on Thresholds (book.gradepro.org)

- Inconsistency severity is judged by whether individual study estimates are separated by one or more pre-established thresholds (trivial, small, moderate, large benefit/harm).
- Important: when a random-effects model is used and the confidence interval already crosses thresholds, reviewers must **avoid double-penalizing** for both inconsistency and imprecision.

## 3. Cochrane Handbook: Heterogeneity Is Real — Represent It, Don't Hide It

### 3.1 Chapter 10 (Analysing Data and Meta-Analyses)

- **Random-effects meta-analysis** should be the default when heterogeneity is present. It provides an estimate of the **average effect across studies**, not a common effect.
- Crucially, the random-effects confidence interval describes uncertainty in the **location of the mean**, not the spread of effects. Citing only this CI when heterogeneity exists is described as **"inappropriate"** (Section 10.10.4.1).
- **Prediction intervals** are the recommended tool for representing the full spread: "a 95% prediction interval estimates where the true effects are to be expected for 95% of similar (exchangeable) studies" (Riley et al., BMJ 2011;342:d549). This directly represents the uncertainty that a simple summary average conceals.
- The Hartung-Knapp-Sidik-Jonkman (HKSJ) adjustment is recommended because it widens the confidence interval to reflect uncertainty in the heterogeneity estimate.

### 3.2 Prediction Intervals in Practice

IntHout et al. (BMJ Open 2016;6:e010247) examined 479 statistically significant Cochrane meta-analyses with heterogeneity: in **72.4%**, the 95% prediction interval suggested the effect could be null or opposite. In **20.3%**, the prediction interval showed the effect could be completely opposite to the point estimate. This demonstrates that failing to report prediction intervals systematically conceals material uncertainty.

### 3.3 Chapter 12: When Meta-Analysis Is Not Appropriate

- When effects are both harmful and beneficial across studies, presenting an average combined effect "can be misleading" (Section 12.1).
- Acceptable alternatives include: structured tabulation, forest plots **without** the summary diamond, harvest plots, and effect direction plots.
- **Vote counting based on statistical significance is unacceptable** (Hedges & Vevea, 1998) because it collapses uncertainty by treating underpowered non-significant studies as showing "no effect."

### 3.4 Chapter 15: Interpreting Results

- "A common mistake is to confuse 'no evidence of an effect' with 'evidence of no effect'" (Section 15.6).
- If the confidence interval overlaps no effect, "the analysis is compatible with both a true beneficial effect and a true harmful effect. If one of the possibilities is mentioned in the conclusion, the other possibility should be mentioned as well."
- Review authors should **not** describe results as "statistically significant" or "non-significant" but should report confidence intervals and exact P values.

## 4. PRISMA 2020: Reporting Requirements That Prevent Collapse of Uncertainty

PRISMA 2020 (Page et al., *PLOS Medicine* 2021) introduced specific items targeting the representation of contradictory findings:

- **Item 13e:** Report "any methods used to explore possible causes of heterogeneity among study results (such as subgroup analysis, meta-regression)."
- **Item 13f:** Report "any sensitivity analyses used to assess robustness of the synthesised results."
- **Item 20c:** "Present results of any investigations of possible causes of heterogeneity among study results."
- **Item 22:** "Present an assessment of certainty (or confidence) in the body of evidence for each outcome assessed."

These items collectively require that heterogeneity and its exploration be made visible to the reader, rather than relegated to a single summary statement.

**Source:** https://journals.plos.org/plosmedicine/article?id=10.1371/journal.pmed.1003583

## 5. SWiM Guideline: Synthesis Without Meta-Analysis

The Synthesis Without Meta-analysis (SWiM) guideline (Campbell et al., *BMJ* 2020;368:l6890) provides nine reporting items for reviews where meta-analysis is not possible or appropriate — a common situation when studies are contradictory.

- **SWiM Item 5 (Investigation of heterogeneity):** Report methods used to examine heterogeneity when meta-analysis is not possible. This includes informal methods such as ordering tables or structuring figures by hypothesized modifiers (study design, population subgroups, intervention components).
- **SWiM Item 7 (Data presentation):** Describe graphical and tabular methods used to present effects, referencing all studies clearly.
- **SWiM Item 8 (Reporting results):** For each comparison and outcome, describe the synthesized findings and indicate which studies contribute, using language consistent with the question and certainty.
- The SWiM guideline explicitly warns against the term "narrative synthesis" without elaboration, as it often conceals methodological choices.

**Source:** https://www.bmj.com/content/368/bmj.l6890

## 6. ICEMAN: Assessing Whether Subgroup Explanations Are Credible

The Instrument for assessing the Credibility of Effect Modification Analyses (ICEMAN; Schandelmaier et al., *CMAJ* 2020;192:E901) provides eight criteria for meta-analyses:

1. Within-trial vs. between-trial comparison (within-trial far more credible)
2. Similarity of effect modification across trials
3. Number of studies (for between-study comparisons)
4. Direction hypothesized a priori
5. Chance as explanation (interaction test p-value)
6. Number of effect modifiers tested (multiplicity)
7. Use of random-effects model
8. Avoidance of arbitrary cut points for continuous modifiers

The overall rating (very low to high credibility) directly determines whether to use **separate subgroup estimates** (moderate/high credibility) or the **overall estimate** (low/very low credibility), explicitly noting remaining uncertainty in both cases.

**Source:** https://iceman.help/manual/meta.html  
**Source:** https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7829020/

## 7. Visual Tools That Preserve Uncertainty

### 7.1 Harvest Plots (Ogilvie et al., *BMC Medical Research Methodology* 2008;8:8)

- Display each study as a bar positioned by effect categorization (beneficial, no effect, harmful).
- Bars are visually weighted and annotated for methodological quality, study design, and sample size.
- Unlike forest plots, harvest plots do not collapse to a single "result" but illustrate the **distribution of evidence** across competing hypotheses.
- Particularly suited to reviews of complex, heterogeneous interventions where meta-analysis is inappropriate.

### 7.2 Effect Direction Plots (Thomson & Thomas, *Research Synthesis Methods* 2013; updated Boon & Thomson, 2021)

- Use arrows to indicate effect direction for each outcome domain within each study.
- The 2019 revision **removed statistical significance** from the algorithm for within-study synthesis — a direct response to the problem of collapsing uncertainty.
- A sign test can be applied to assess whether the pattern of effect directions across studies is compatible with chance, but the plot itself preserves visibility of all contradictory findings.

**Source:** https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/1471-2288-8-8  
**Source:** https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7821279/

## 8. Summary of the Recommended Approach

A systematic review facing materially contradictory primary studies should:

1. **Not suppress the contradiction.** Present all individual study results transparently (forest plot without summary diamond if necessary; harvest or effect direction plots).

2. **Use random-effects meta-analysis with a prediction interval**, not merely a confidence interval for the average effect. The prediction interval quantifies the range of effects compatible with the evidence.

3. **Apply GRADE inconsistency assessment** using pre-established thresholds. If studies fall on opposite sides of a decision threshold, rate down certainty.

4. **Test pre-specified subgroup hypotheses** (≤3, directional) and assess their credibility with ICEMAN. If credible, present separate evidence profiles for each subgroup. If not credible, report the overall estimate with downgraded certainty.

5. **Distinguish "no evidence of effect" from "evidence of no effect."** When confidence intervals span both benefit and harm, explicitly state that both are compatible with the data.

6. **Report methods transparently** per PRISMA 2020 (items 13e, 13f, 20c, 22) and, when meta-analysis is not used, per SWiM (especially items 5, 7, and 8).

7. **Avoid all forms of invalid vote counting** (based on statistical significance) and avoid presenting only a single narrative conclusion when the underlying evidence is materially contradictory.

# How a Systematic Review Should Represent Materially Contradictory Primary Studies Without Collapsing Uncertainty: An Analyst's Framework

## 1. The Problem and Its Prevalence

When primary studies in a systematic review point in opposite directions or show substantially different magnitudes of effect, the dominant risk is that a single pooled estimate—or a single narrative conclusion—conceals genuine uncertainty. This is not a marginal problem: IntHout et al. (BMJ Open 2016) examined 479 statistically significant Cochrane meta-analyses with any heterogeneity (I² > 0) and found that in **72.4%** the 95% prediction interval spanned the null, and in **20.3%** it included effects opposite to the point estimate. These are cases where conventional reporting (point estimate + confidence interval) systematically misrepresented the evidence.

The authoritative guidance—GRADE, Cochrane Handbook, PRISMA 2020, SWiM, and ICEMAN—converges on a common principle: **uncertainty must be represented structurally, not relegated to a caveat in the Discussion.** This means using specific quantitative tools, visual methods, and analytical frameworks that keep plurality visible.

## 2. The Core Framework: Four Interlocking Strategies

### 2.1 Strategy A: Quantify the Spread, Not Just the Average

The most fundamental failure is quoting only the random-effects summary estimate and its confidence interval. The Cochrane Handbook (Ch. 10, §10.10.4.1) states explicitly that this is "inappropriate" because the random-effects CI describes uncertainty in the *location of the mean*, not the spread of true effects across studies.

**Required: Prediction Intervals (PIs).**
- A 95% PI estimates the range within which the true effect of a future exchangeable study would fall (Riley et al., BMJ 2011;342:d549).
- PIs are computed from the summary estimate and the between-study variance (τ²). In RevMan (2024+), they are available using REML estimation with the HKSJ adjustment.
- The PI directly answers the question: "If I apply this intervention in my setting, what range of effects is compatible with the evidence?"
- **Caveat:** PIs assume a normal distribution of effects and require ≥5 studies for reasonable precision (Partlett & Riley 2017). When fewer studies exist, authors should report τ and its confidence interval and explicitly state that the PI would be unreliable.

**Supplementary: HKSJ Confidence Intervals.** The Hartung-Knapp-Sidik-Jonkman adjustment widens the CI to reflect uncertainty in τ² estimation. Cochrane now recommends it over the Wald-type CI.

### 2.2 Strategy B: Apply GRADE Inconsistency Assessment Using Pre-established Thresholds

The GRADE Working Group's 2025 Core GRADE 3 paper (BMJ 2025;389:e081905) and the GRADE guidance 36 update (Guyatt et al., J Clin Epidemiol 2023) provide a structured three-step judgment:

**Step 1 — Three Visual Criteria:**
1. **Magnitude of differences in point estimates** across studies.
2. **Overlap of confidence intervals** — completely non-overlapping CIs provide a strong rationale for rating down.
3. **Relation of point estimates to the threshold of certainty rating** (the null, or the minimal important difference [MID]). If all point estimates fall on the same side of the threshold, inconsistency may not be problematic even when I² is high. If they fall on opposite sides, inconsistency is likely important.

**Step 2 — Interpret I² Cautiously.** The I² statistic is a complement, not a rule. High I² with all point estimates on one side of the MID may not warrant rating down. I² can also be misleadingly high when studies have very narrow CIs, or misleadingly low when CIs are wide.

**Step 3 — Rate Down If Unexplained.** If visual inspection confirms problematic inconsistency and a priori hypotheses cannot explain it, rate down certainty (typically one level; rating down twice is theoretically possible but "sufficiently unusual that it need not concern users of Core GRADE").

**Critical insight from the GRADE guidance:** The same body of evidence can yield *different* inconsistency judgments depending on the threshold chosen. For example, in the total knee arthroplasty analgesia meta-analysis (Fig. 4 of Core GRADE 3), 12 of 13 studies favoured the intervention — no inconsistency if the threshold is the null. But only 8 of 13 exceeded the MID of 10 mm on a 100 mm VAS, warranting rating down for inconsistency when the MID is the target. This means reviewers must make their threshold explicit and justify it.

### 2.3 Strategy C: Test Credible Subgroup Hypotheses, and Act on the Result

The GRADE approach requires constructing **a small number (≤3) of directional a priori hypotheses** about what might explain inconsistency — specified before seeing results and including the expected direction of effect.

**Assess credibility with ICEMAN** (Schandelmaier et al., CMAJ 2020;192:E901). The instrument scores eight criteria:

| Criterion | Key Consideration |
|---|---|
| 1. Within- vs. between-study comparison | Within-trial comparisons are far more credible (no study-level confounding) |
| 2. Similarity across trials | Is the effect modification replicated? |
| 3. Number of studies | ≥5 per subgroup is "rather large" for subgroup analysis |
| 4. Direction hypothesized a priori | Was it pre-specified with the correct direction? |
| 5. Interaction test P value | P ≤ 0.005 is "chance an unlikely explanation" |
| 6. Number of modifiers tested | ≤3 pre-specified is "definitely yes" for credibility |
| 7. Random-effects model used | Required; common/fixed effect models inflate false positives |
| 8. Avoidance of arbitrary cut points | Continuous variables should not be arbitrarily dichotomized |

**The decision rule from ICEMAN:**
- **Very low or low credibility** → use the overall estimate; note remaining uncertainty.
- **Moderate or high credibility** → present separate evidence summaries and separate GRADE certainty ratings for each subgroup.
- **Close to the low/moderate boundary** → acknowledge this explicitly; consider presenting both overall and subgroup results.

This is the mechanism by which GRADE avoids collapsing uncertainty: if a credible explanation for contradictory findings exists, the review *splits* into multiple evidence profiles rather than averaging across them.

### 2.4 Strategy D: When Meta-Analysis Is Inappropriate, Use Structured Alternatives That Preserve Plurality

The Cochrane Handbook (Ch. 12, §12.1) states that when effects are both harmful and beneficial across studies, presenting an average combined effect "can be misleading." The SWiM guideline (Campbell et al., BMJ 2020;368:l6890) provides nine reporting items for synthesis without meta-analysis. Key requirements:

- **SWiM Item 5:** Report methods for investigating heterogeneity (e.g., ordering tables by hypothesized modifiers such as study design, population, or intervention components).
- **SWiM Item 7:** Describe graphical and tabular methods. Acceptable tools include:
  - **Harvest plots** (Ogilvie et al., BMC Med Res Methodol 2008): Each study is a bar positioned by effect category (beneficial, no effect, harmful), weighted and annotated for quality and design. No summary diamond.
  - **Effect direction plots** (Boon & Thomson, Res Synth Methods 2021): Arrows for each outcome domain within each study. The 2019 revision removed statistical significance from the algorithm.
  - **Forest plots without the summary diamond**, with studies ordered by hypothesized effect modifiers.
- **SWiM Item 8:** Describe findings in language consistent with the question the synthesis addresses, making clear which studies contribute.

**What SWiM explicitly rejects:** Vote counting based on statistical significance (Hedges & Vevea 1998), and the opaque use of the term "narrative synthesis" without elaboration of methods.

## 3. The Language Discipline: How to Write About Contradictory Evidence

The Cochrane Handbook (Ch. 15, §15.6) and GRADE both prescribe specific linguistic practices that prevent the collapse of uncertainty:

1. **Distinguish "no evidence of an effect" from "evidence of no effect."** If the CI overlaps the null, "the analysis is compatible with both a true beneficial effect and a true harmful effect. If one of the possibilities is mentioned in the conclusion, the other possibility should be mentioned as well."

2. **Avoid statistical significance language.** Do not describe results as "statistically significant" or "non-significant." Report CIs and exact P values.

3. **Avoid "evidence of no effect" phrasing.** GRADE advises that evidence is a continuum, not a binary classification.

4. **When using prediction intervals:** State explicitly what range of effects is compatible with the evidence in future settings. For example: "The 95% prediction interval ranges from a risk ratio of 0.81 to 4.72, indicating that in some settings the intervention may confer no benefit or even harm."

## 4. Structural Decisions: When to Split vs. When to Downgrade

The following decision logic synthesizes GRADE, Cochrane, and ICEMAN guidance:

```
                    ┌─── All point estimates on same side of threshold?
                    │
                    ├─ YES → No rating down for inconsistency.
                    │        (Even if I² is high; variability in PICO
                    │         elements enhances applicability.)
                    │
                    ├─ NO (point estimates on opposite sides) →
                    │        │
                    │        ├─ Credible a priori subgroup hypothesis
                    │        │  (ICEMAN: moderate/high credibility)?
                    │        │     │
                    │        │     ├─ YES → Split: separate evidence
                    │        │     │  profiles, separate GRADE ratings
                    │        │     │  per subgroup. Report prediction
                    │        │     │  intervals within each subgroup.
                    │        │     │
                    │        │     └─ NO → Rate down one level for
                    │        │        inconsistency. Present overall
                    │        │        estimate with prediction interval.
                    │        │        State explicitly: "both benefit
                    │        │        and harm are compatible with
                    │        │        the evidence."
                    │        │
                    │        └─ If meta-analysis is impossible or
                    │           inappropriate → SWiM approach:
                    │           harvest/effect direction plots,
                    │           structured tabulation, no summary
                    │           diamond. Report per SWiM items 1-9.
```

## 5. Common Mistakes That Collapse Uncertainty (And How to Guard Against Them)

| Mistake | Consequence | Correct Approach |
|---|---|---|
| Reporting only the random-effects CI without a prediction interval | Conceals the spread of true effects; 72% of significant meta-analyses with heterogeneity have PIs crossing the null | Always report PI when ≥5 studies are available |
| Vote counting based on statistical significance | Treats underpowered non-significant studies as showing "no effect" | Use direction-of-effect vote counting if needed; never based on significance |
| Using "narrative synthesis" without specifying methods | Obscures whether plurality was preserved or collapsed | Follow SWiM items 1–9 |
| Presenting only one side of the CI in conclusions | Implies certainty where there is none | Explicitly state both compatible possibilities |
| Testing many post-hoc subgroups | Inflates false positive findings | Limit to ≤3 directional a priori hypotheses |
| Treating I² as a diagnostic threshold | Ignores that I² depends on precision, not just heterogeneity | Use visual criteria + threshold analysis; I² is supplementary |
| Failing to specify the MID threshold for inconsistency assessment | Different thresholds yield different inconsistency judgments | Pre-specify and justify the MID |

## 6. The Reporting Checklist (PRISMA 2020 + SWiM)

- **PRISMA Item 13e:** Methods to explore heterogeneity (subgroup analysis, meta-regression).
- **PRISMA Item 13f:** Sensitivity analyses for robustness.
- **PRISMA Item 20c:** Results of heterogeneity investigations.
- **PRISMA Item 22:** Certainty assessment for each outcome.
- **SWiM Items 1–9:** When meta-analysis is not used: grouping rationale, metric, synthesis method, prioritization criteria, heterogeneity investigation, certainty methods, data presentation, results reporting, and synthesis limitations.

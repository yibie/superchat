# Evidence Assessment: Do Replication Attempts Improve the Reliability of a Research Field?

**Assignment:** disc-science-03 | **Family:** scientific-evidence | **Role:** analyst

---

## 1. Overview

The claim that replication attempts improve the reliability of a research field is supported by strong empirical evidence from large-scale replication projects but is also seriously challenged by philosophical arguments and methodological critiques. The evidence supports a nuanced conclusion: replication is *necessary but not sufficient* — it improves reliability most when embedded in broader structural reforms (preregistration, large samples, Registered Reports, transparency) rather than as a standalone corrective. Below, evidence is organized into **Supporting** evidence and **Challenging** evidence, each with sub-themes, followed by a synthesis.

---

## 2. Evidence SUPPORTING That Replication Improves Reliability

### 2.1 Large-Scale Replication Projects Expose Inflated Effect Sizes and Unreliable Findings

The most direct empirical evidence comes from systematic replication projects:

- **Reproducibility Project: Psychology (OSC, 2015):** 100 studies from three top journals were replicated. Replication effect sizes were roughly *half* the magnitude of original effects (Mr = 0.197 vs. 0.403). Only 36% of replications were statistically significant vs. 97% of originals. Only 39% were subjectively rated as successful ([Science, DOI: 10.1126/science.aac4716](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.aac4716)).

- **Reproducibility Project: Cancer Biology (Errington et al., 2021):** Across 158 effects from 23 high-impact papers, replication effect sizes were 85% smaller than original findings (median original = 2.96 vs. replication = 0.43). Original positive results replicated successfully on most criteria only 40% of the time, vs. 80% for null results ([eLife, DOI: 10.7554/eLife.67995](https://doi.org/10.7554/eLife.67995)). Only 6 of 56 landmark preclinical cancer studies (11%) were replicated by Amgen scientists ([Begley & Ellis 2012, *Nature*](https://doi.org/10.1038/483531a)). Pharmaceutical companies reported inability to replicate the majority of academic preclinical literature ([Prinz et al., 2011, *Nature Reviews Drug Discovery*](https://doi.org/10.1038/nrd3439-c1)).

**Analyst assessment:** These projects provide *direct empirical evidence* that replication functions as a diagnostic tool, identifying which findings are overestimated or unreliable. Without replication, inflated effects would continue to anchor subsequent research, wasting resources and misleading theory development. The systematic nature of these projects — with pre-registered protocols, original author consultation, and high statistical power — strengthens causal attribution: the replication process *itself* exposed the unreliability.

### 2.2 Replication Diagnoses Systematic Reporting Failures and Fraud

The Cancer Biology project revealed that *none* of the 193 original experiments were described in sufficient detail to permit replication without contacting original authors; 68% could not provide key data even when contacted ([Buck 2024, *HDSR*](https://hdsr.mitpress.mit.edu/pub/4brnqfcf/release/1)). Replication scrutiny also facilitated detection of fraud (e.g., the Lesné et al. 2006 Alzheimer's study in *Nature*, found likely fraudulent after systematic investigation) ([Piller 2022, *Science*](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.add9993)).

### 2.3 Replication Can Validate Robust Effects When Best Practices Are Followed

**Many Labs 1 (Klein et al., 2014):** 13 classic psychology effects replicated across 36 samples (N = 6,344); 11 of 13 replicated successfully. **Many Labs 2 (N = 15,305, 125 samples, 36 countries):** 54% showed significant effects in the same direction; variability was attributable more to the effect studied than to sample or setting ([OSF](https://osf.io/8cd4r/)).

**Protzko et al. (2020/2024):** A prospective replication study across four independent laboratories using preregistration, large samples (N ≈ 1,500+), and methodological transparency found that 86% of replication attempts were successful, with replication effect sizes at 97% of original effect sizes. This established a benchmark that high replicability is achievable when rigor-enhancing practices are used from the start ([*Nature Human Behaviour*](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41562-023-01749-9); **note:** this paper was retracted in September 2024). However, the broader point — that best practices improve replicability — is corroborated by Nosek et al. (2022), who reviewed 307 replications and found that 64% reported statistically significant evidence in the same direction, with effect sizes 68% as large as originals ([*Annual Review of Psychology*](https://doi.org/10.1146/annurev-psych-020821-114157)).

### 2.4 Preregistration Combined with Replication Reduces Bias

Kvarven, Strømland & Johannesson (2020, *Nature Human Behaviour*) compared 15 preregistered multi-lab replications with corresponding meta-analyses: meta-analyses and replications agreed on directional conclusions only 47% of the time; meta-analytic effect sizes were significantly larger for 80% of pairs. Medicine — which requires trial registration — showed smaller systematic differences, suggesting preregistration-plus-replication reduces bias ([Replication Network summary](https://replicationnetwork.com/2020/01/02/what-do-replications-tell-us-about-the-reliability-of-meta-analyses-evidence-from-psychology/)).

### 2.5 Bayesian Reanalysis Shows Replications Produce Consistent Evidence

Etz & Vandekerckhove (2016, *PLOS ONE*) reanalyzed OSC (2015) data using Bayes factors: 75% of studies gave qualitatively similar evidential results. No case showed strong support for a hypothesis by one team and contradiction by another. Apparent failures were largely attributable to small samples and publication bias in originals, not genuine contradictions ([DOI: 10.1371/journal.pone.0149794](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0149794)).

### 2.6 The Credibility Revolution: Structural Reforms Motivated by Replication Failures

Korbmacher et al. (2023, *Communications Psychology*) argue that the replication crisis has driven a "credibility revolution": positive structural, procedural, and community changes (Registered Reports, TOP Guidelines, preregistration norms, open data) have transformed research practices. These reforms would not have occurred without large-scale replication projects demonstrating low replicability ([*Communications Psychology*](https://preview-www.nature.com/articles/s44271-023-00003-2)). Korbmacher & Schöberlein (2024, *PMC*) defend the "orthodox view" that replications are *always* epistemically useful: "we cannot rely on a single replication just as we cannot rely on any single experiment. These difficulties are ameliorated by multiplying replications. As long as those replications are of acceptable methodological quality, the combined results will converge toward the truth" ([PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11569024/)).

### 2.7 Replication Improves Reliability by Revealing Hidden Moderators

Buck (2024) argues that failed replications can reveal "seemingly insignificant factors" that moderate effects — as when Hines et al. (2014) discovered that *stirring rate* of tissue samples affected breast cancer cell results. Without direct replication attempts, such hidden moderators remain undiscovered, and the field cannot calibrate its understanding of boundary conditions ([*HDSR*](https://hdsr.mitpress.mit.edu/pub/4brnqfcf/release/1)).

## 3. Evidence CHALLENGING That Replication Improves Reliability

### 3.1 The Experimenter's Regress (Collins, 1985/2016)

Harry Collins argued that replication cannot settle disputes because determining whether a replication was *properly conducted* depends on knowing the correct result — which is what the replication is meant to discover. This circularity means failed replications can always be attributed to replicator incompetence or missing tacit knowledge rather than to a false original finding ([Collins 2016, *Wiley*](https://doi.org/10.1002/9781118865064.ch4)). Mitchell (2014, Harvard) argued that "failed replications" may be "evidentially empty" because eliciting a positive effect requires tacit, unwritten knowledge — like following a recipe without knowing what "medium heat" means ([Buck 2024](https://hdsr.mitpress.mit.edu/pub/4brnqfcf/release/1)).

**Analyst note:** Franklin (1994) rebutted Collins with evidence from the gravitational-wave controversy, arguing that "epistemological criteria were reasonably applied" and the regress was broken by *reasoned argument*, not mere negotiation. The debate remains unresolved. This challenge is plausible for high-tacit-knowledge domains but less applicable to fields where methods are highly standardized.

### 3.2 Meta-Analysis as a Replication Success Metric Performs Poorly

Muradchanian et al. (2024, *PLOS ONE*) evaluated meta-analysis as a replication success metric using data from SCORE and OSC projects. They found meta-analysis "performed poorly" — in many cases, it deemed replications successful *regardless of the replication results*, because the original study's significance drove the pooled estimate. Meta-analysis has "a relatively high probability of finding evidence in favour of a non-zero population effect even when it is zero," especially when the replication-to-original sample-size ratio is low. A 5:1 ratio may be necessary for diagnostic value ([PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11633967/)).

### 3.3 Irreducible Heterogeneity Undermines Simple Replicability Assessments

McShane et al. (2019, *The American Statistician*) argue that current replication projects are designed to *eliminate* heterogeneity, yet the nontrivial heterogeneity found represents a *lower bound* on the true degree. "If effect sizes are sufficiently heterogeneous from one study to the next — even if the sign of the effect is consistent — the phenomenon in question does not seem particularly replicable and the theory underlying it seems poorly constructed" ([DOI: 10.1080/00031305.2018.1505655](https://doi.org/10.1080/00031305.2018.1505655)). A 2025 *PLOS ONE* meta-regression study confirmed that between-study-pair heterogeneity is often ignored when replication results are summarized, "which led some to criticize the methodology employed by RPs as they tend to draw negative conclusions" ([DOI: 10.1371/journal.pone.0327799](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0327799)).

### 3.4 Legitimate Disagreement About Replication Success

Trautmann (2025, *Journal for General Philosophy of Science*) argues that evaluating replication is fundamentally *not black-and-white*: "it is a complex process that necessarily involves normative judgment-making and naturally leads to disagreements." Researchers may legitimately disagree because they assign different epistemic functions to replications (controlling for sampling error vs. testing generalizability), and no single marker — p-value, effect size, subjective rating — yields the same conclusion ([DOI: 10.1007/s10838-024-09709-1](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10838-024-09709-1)). This undermines the claim that replication *straightforwardly* improves reliability.

### 3.5 Replication May Not Be Central to Research Progress

Feest (2019, *Philosophy of Science*) argues that direct replication can address random but not systematic error, since any replication inherits the same tacit assumptions and potential biases as the original. Conceptual replication "begs the question" because it presupposes a good grasp of the relevant concepts — exactly what research aims to establish. She contends much psychological research is exploratory rather than confirmatory, and that the "crisis" is better described as one of questionable statistical practices rather than failed replication per se ([DOI: 10.1086/705451](https://doi.org/10.1086/705451)). Irvine (2021) adds that without well-developed background theories (often absent in psychology), replication provides limited theoretical insight.

### 3.6 Preregistration Alone May Not Prevent QRPs

Van den Akker et al. (2023, *Behavior Research Methods*) compared 193 preregistered studies with matched non-preregistered studies: they found *no* lower proportion of positive results or smaller effect sizes in preregistered studies, and *no* fewer statistical inconsistencies. They did find larger sample sizes and more power analyses in preregistered studies. This suggests preregistration alone, without additional structural reforms (e.g., Registered Reports), may not fully deliver the reliability benefits attributed to it ([DOI: 10.3758/s13428-023-02277-0](https://doi.org/10.3758/s13428-023-02277-0)).

### 3.7 Replicating Systematic Reviews Remains Problematic

Hamilton et al. (2025, *Research Synthesis Methods*) independently replicated literature searches and meta-analyses of 10 systematic reviews of health interventions. Initially, 80% of meta-analyses were classified as "not fully replicable." After resolving discrepancies, this fell to 50%, though only 0–10% were "meaningfully different" in direction or statistical significance. This demonstrates that even the synthesis methods meant to aggregate replication evidence are themselves imperfectly replicable, complicating the claim that replication cleanly improves reliability ([DOI: 10.1017/rsm.2025.10004](https://doi.org/10.1017/rsm.2025.10004)).

## 4. Synthesis and Analyst Judgment

The evidence supports the following balanced conclusion:

**Replication *does* improve reliability**, as demonstrated by: (a) systematic exposure of inflated effect sizes (OSC 2015, Errington et al. 2021); (b) empirical demonstration that effects can be validated when rigor-enhancing practices are followed (Many Labs, Nosek et al. 2022); (c) diagnostic functions that expose fraud, QRPs, and under-specified methods (Buck 2024); (d) Bayesian analyses showing that replication *clarifies* rather than destabilizes the evidence base (Etz & Vandekerckhove 2016); and (e) driving the credibility revolution that has structurally reformed research practices (Korbmacher et al. 2023).

**Replication's reliability-enhancing function is challenged** by: (a) the experimenter's regress and tacit knowledge arguments (Collins, Mitchell) — though these are partly rebutted by Franklin and by the empirical success of well-specified replication protocols; (b) the poor performance of meta-analysis as a replication metric (Muradchanian et al. 2024); (c) irreducible between-study heterogeneity that resists binary success/failure judgments (McShane et al. 2019); (d) normative disagreement about what counts as success (Trautmann 2025); and (e) evidence that preregistration alone may be insufficient to prevent QRPs (Van den Akker et al. 2023).

**The weight of evidence favors a conditional view:** replication improves reliability most when it is (1) combined with preregistration, large samples, and transparency; (2) understood through continuous-evidential rather than binary-significance frameworks; (3) treated as one component of a broader package of structural reforms (Registered Reports, open data, methodological training); and (4) interpreted with awareness of heterogeneity and potential tacit-knowledge barriers, particularly in fields with low standardization. Replication is necessary but not sufficient — it functions as a diagnostic and corrective mechanism that works *with*, not in place of, rigorous primary research practices.

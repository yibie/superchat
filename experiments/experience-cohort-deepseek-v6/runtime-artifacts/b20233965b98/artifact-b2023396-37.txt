# Evidence Assessment: Do Replication Attempts Improve the Reliability of a Research Field?

**Assignment:** disc-science-03 | **Family:** scientific-evidence | **Role:** collector

---

## 1. Evidence SUPPORTING That Replication Improves Reliability

### 1.1 Large-Scale Replication Projects Expose Inflated Effect Sizes

The Reproducibility Project: Psychology (Open Science Collaboration, 2015) attempted direct replications of 100 studies from three top psychology journals. Only 36–47% replicated successfully across different metrics. Original effect sizes were roughly double those in replications. This empirical demonstration of effect-size inflation provides direct evidence that replication identifies unreliable findings, enabling the field to recalibrate confidence ([OSC 2015, *Science*](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.aac4716); [Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy §2.1](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/scientific-reproducibility/)).

The Reproducibility Project: Cancer Biology found that replication effect sizes were 85% smaller on average than original findings; original positive results replicated successfully only 40% of the time, versus 80% for original null results ([Errington et al., 2021, *eLife*](https://doi.org/10.7554/eLife.67995)). Begley & Ellis (2012, *Nature*) reported that only 6 of 56 landmark preclinical cancer studies (11%) could be replicated by Amgen scientists.

Pharmaceutical companies Prinz et al. (2011, *Nature Reviews Drug Discovery*) reported inability to replicate much of the academic preclinical literature despite strong commercial incentives to do so, suggesting the replication process distinguishes robust from unreliable findings ([Buck 2024, *HDSR*](https://hdsr.mitpress.mit.edu/pub/4brnqfcf/release/1)).

### 1.2 Many Labs Projects Demonstrate That Replication Can Validate Robust Effects

The Many Labs project (Klein et al., 2014) replicated 13 classic psychology phenomena across 36 independent samples (N = 6,344), finding that 11 of 13 effects replicated successfully. Many Labs 2 (N = 15,305, 125 samples, 36 countries) replicated 28 effects: 54% showed statistically significant effects in the same direction as originals; 50% held even at p < .0001. Critically, the project demonstrated that variability in effect sizes was attributable more to the *effect being studied* than to sample or setting, indicating that replication can distinguish genuinely robust phenomena from fragile ones ([Many Labs 2, OSF](https://osf.io/8cd4r/)).

### 1.3 Replication Exposes Frauds, QRPs, and Methodological Failures

Buck (2024) documents that replication attempts in the Cancer Biology project revealed that "none of the 193 experiments were described in sufficient detail in the original paper" — in every case the team had to contact original labs, and 68% could not obtain key data. This diagnostic function alone improves a field by exposing systematic reporting failures ([Buck 2024, *HDSR*](https://hdsr.mitpress.mit.edu/pub/4brnqfcf/release/1)).

Replication also detects fraud: the prominent Alzheimer's study by Lesné et al. (2006, *Nature*) was found likely fraudulent only after systematic scrutiny — a process that replication projects facilitate ([Piller 2022, *Science*](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.add9993), cited in Buck 2024).

### 1.4 Replication Combined with Best Practices Yields High Replicability

Protzko et al. (2020) implemented best practices (preregistration, large samples, shared materials) in a round-robin replication design across four independent laboratories. Replication effect sizes were 97% as large as the original studies, demonstrating that when rigorous practices are followed, replication reliably confirms findings ([Nosek et al. 2022, *Annual Review of Psychology*](https://www.annualreviews.org/doi/10.1146/annurev-psych-020821-114157)).

### 1.5 Preregistration and Replication Synergistically Improve Reliability

Kvarven, Strømland & Johannesson (2020, *Nature Human Behaviour*) compared 15 preregistered multi-laboratory replication studies with 15 corresponding meta-analyses in psychology. Meta-analyses and replications reached the same directional conclusion only 47% of the time; for 80% of study pairs, the meta-analytic effect size was significantly larger (inflated). They note that medicine — which requires trial registration — shows smaller systematic differences between meta-analyses and large trials, suggesting that preregistration-plus-replication reduces bias ([Replication Network summary](https://replicationnetwork.com/2020/01/02/what-do-replications-tell-us-about-the-reliability-of-meta-analyses-evidence-from-psychology/)).

### 1.6 Bayesian Reanalysis Confirms Replications Produce Consistent Evidence

Etz & Vandekerckhove (2016, *PLOS ONE*) reanalyzed OSC (2015) data using Bayes factors and concluded that 75% of studies gave qualitatively similar results in terms of evidence, with no case where an hypothesis was strongly supported by one team but contradicted by another. Apparent failures were largely explained by small samples and publication bias in originals — not by genuine contradictions, strengthening the case that systematic replication clarifies, rather than merely destabilizes, the evidence base ([Etz & Vandekerckhove 2016](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0149794)).

### 1.7 Theoretical Argument: Replications as Epistemically Essential

Korbmacher & Schöberlein (2024, *PMC*) defend the "orthodox view" that all replications — direct and conceptual — provide additional evidence and are epistemically useful. They argue that "we cannot rely on a single replication just as we cannot rely on any single experiment. These difficulties are ameliorated by multiplying replications. As long as those replications are of acceptable methodological quality, the combined results will converge toward the truth" ([PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11569024/)).

## 2. Evidence CHALLENGING That Replication Improves Reliability

### 2.1 The Experimenter's Regress

Harry Collins (1985, 2016) argued that replication cannot settle scientific disputes because determining whether a replication was properly conducted itself depends on knowing the correct result — which is what the replication is meant to discover. This circularity (the "experimenter's regress") means that failed replications can always be attributed to replicator incompetence or missing tacit knowledge rather than to a false original finding ([Collins 2016, *Wiley*](https://doi.org/10.1002/9781118865064.ch4); [Wikipedia summary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Experimenter's_regress)).

Mitchell (2014, Harvard) argued that "failed replications" may be "evidentially empty" because eliciting a positive effect requires tacit, unwritten knowledge — like following a recipe without knowing what "medium heat" means. If replicators lack this tacit skill, failure says nothing about the original finding ([Buck 2024 quotes Mitchell](https://hdsr.mitpress.mit.edu/pub/4brnqfcf/release/1)).

### 2.2 Meta-Analysis as a Replication Metric Performs Poorly

Muradchanian et al. (2024, *PLOS ONE*) evaluated meta-analysis as a replication success metric using data from the SCORE and OSC projects. They found that "meta-analysis performed poorly when used as a replication success metric. In many cases, quantifying replication success using meta-analysis resulted in the conclusion where the replication was deemed a success regardless of the results of the replication study." Specifically, meta-analysis has "a relatively high probability of finding evidence in favour of a non-zero population effect even when it is zero" — largely because the original study's significance drives the pooled estimate ([PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11633967/)).

### 2.3 Heterogeneity Undermines Simple Replicability Assessments

McShane et al. (2019, *The American Statistician*) argue that current large-scale replication projects provide only limited information because they are designed to eliminate heterogeneity, yet the nontrivial heterogeneity found represents a *lower bound* on the true degree. "If effect sizes are sufficiently heterogeneous from one study to the next — even if the sign of the effect is consistent — the phenomenon in question does not seem particularly replicable and the theory underlying it seems poorly constructed." They recommend embracing heterogeneity rather than treating replication as a binary outcome ([McShane et al., *Tandfonline*](https://doi.org/10.1080/00031305.2018.1505655)).

A 2025 PLOS ONE study on meta-regression confirmed that between-study-pair heterogeneity is often ignored when replication project results are summarized, "which led some to criticize the methodology employed by RPs as they tend to draw negative conclusions with respect to the reproducibility of the investigated research fields" ([PLOS ONE 2025](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0327799)).

### 2.4 Disagreement About Replication Success Is Inevitable

Trautmann (2025, *Journal for General Philosophy of Science*) argues that evaluating replication is fundamentally not a black-and-white undertaking: "it is a complex process that necessarily involves normative judgment-making and naturally leads to disagreements." Researchers may legitimately disagree because they assign different epistemic functions to replications (e.g., controlling for sampling error vs. testing generalizability), and no single marker — p-value, effect size, subjective rating — yields the same conclusion. This undermines the claim that replication straightforwardly improves reliability ([Springer Link](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10838-024-09709-1)).

### 2.5 Replication May Not Be Central to Research Progress

Feest (2019, *Philosophy of Science*) argues both direct and conceptual replication have limited epistemic value in psychology. Direct replication can address random error but not systematic error; conceptual replication "begs the question" because it presupposes a good grasp of the relevant concepts — exactly what research aims to establish. She contends that much psychological research is exploratory rather than confirmatory, and that the "crisis" is better described as one of questionable statistical practices rather than failed replication per se ([Feest 2019](https://doi.org/10.1086/705451)).

### 2.6 Empirical Evidence of Limited Predictive Power

The repliCATS project (SCORE program) used structured expert elicitation to predict replication outcomes. Even statistical models showed that if the true population effect is zero, replications still have a high likelihood of appearing successful through a meta-analytic lens unless replication sample sizes are much larger than originals (5:1 ratio needed) — limiting replication's diagnostic value under typical conditions ([Muradchanian et al. 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11633967/)).

### 2.7 Pervasive QRPs and Publication Bias Resist Correction

The Stanford Encyclopedia notes that publication bias toward positive results reaches ~90%+ in many disciplines despite average statistical power of only ~23–44%. This systematic distortion means that even when replications are conducted, the published literature remains heavily skewed, potentially limiting the corrective power of replication alone without structural reforms to incentives ([Stanford Encyclopedia §2.2–2.3](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/scientific-reproducibility/)).

## 3. Synthesis

The evidence supports a nuanced picture. Replication *does* improve reliability when it: (a) exposes inflated effect sizes and fraud; (b) identifies which findings are robust across contexts (Many Labs); (c) is combined with preregistration and large samples (Protzko et al.); and (d) is understood through Bayesian rather than dichotomous NHST frameworks (Etz & Vandekerckhove). However, replication's reliability-enhancing function is challenged by: (a) the experimenter's regress and tacit knowledge (Collins, Mitchell); (b) poor performance of meta-analysis as a success metric (Muradchanian et al.); (c) irreducible heterogeneity (McShane et al.); (d) normative disagreement about success criteria (Trautmann); and (e) the argument that replication is less central to research than exploration (Feest). The weight of evidence suggests replication is necessary but not sufficient — it improves reliability most when embedded in broader structural reforms (preregistration, Registered Reports, Bayesian methods, larger samples) rather than as a standalone corrective mechanism.
